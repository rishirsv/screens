# Layout and type

Start with the primary task and reading order. Put related items close together and separate groups with more space than the space within them. Use a few shared alignment axes. Cards suit independently meaningful items; avoid nesting them. An interface needs one clear primary action, not a catalog of features. Match density to the task: expert workflows may need more visible information than onboarding.

For repeated rows, reserve fixed-width, non-shrinking slots for icons, indicators and trailing actions, even where a row lacks one. Trace vertical lanes across three or more rows. Align text and controls optically after their structural edges align. Let content define breakpoints: stack or reflow when it stops fitting, preserve reading order and keep actions reachable. Allow long strings to wrap; avoid fixed heights that clip content.

Choose one or two complementary type families, a small scale and distinct roles. Product UI can use its established or platform system type; an expressive headline can carry a marketing page. Keep body lines comfortably readable, generally under 80 characters, and give text enough line height. Use weight and size for hierarchy before adding labels. Avoid small text under 16px unless the context warrants it, especially 12px or less outside dense productivity UI or a deliberate all-caps treatment. Check small text contrast and wrapping in the actual rendering. Use tabular figures for changing or aligned numbers.

Write as the person using the product would speak. Name actions consistently, use active verbs and sentence case, and cut text that merely restates the visible layout. Do not invent factual proof, product claims or endorsements.
